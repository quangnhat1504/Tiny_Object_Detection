\begin{abstract}
Tiny object detection is bottlenecked by the similarity metric used for
label assignment, bounding-box regression, and non-maximum suppression. For
objects spanning fewer than 32 pixels, Intersection over Union exhibits
near-binary sensitivity to sub-pixel shifts, starving tiny ground truths of
positive anchors and introducing noisy regression gradients. Gaussian
Wasserstein surrogates restore smoothness by modeling boxes as two-dimensional
Gaussian distributions, but they share two structural weaknesses: shape
deviation is measured in Euclidean space, which is not scale invariant, and
position errors along both axes are normalized by a single isotropic
denominator, which cannot encode the per-axis tolerances of elongated objects
such as standing persons or vessels. We propose the Anisotropic
Log-Wasserstein distance, which normalizes horizontal and vertical position
errors with per-axis root-mean-square denominators and measures shape
deviation as absolute log-ratios of width and height (penalized with a Charbonnier
robust loss for numerical stability). We prove that ALW is
symmetric, dimensionally consistent, and exactly scale invariant---the first
Gaussian Wasserstein metric to combine per-axis anisotropy with a
log-ratio shape measure. As a parameter-free drop-in replacement for IoU in receptive-field label
assignment and bounding-box regression, ALW is evaluated on a maritime
tiny-person dataset under a controlled training harness. Internal ablation
confirms that anisotropy and log-ratio shape each contribute independently,
and ALW is competitive with its direct predecessor while closing the two
structural defects.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
tiny object detection, bounding-box metric, Wasserstein distance, label

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Object detectors have matured on benchmarks dominated by medium and large
objects, yet their accuracy collapses when the target spans only a handful of
pixels. Tiny object detection is central to maritime search-and-rescue, aerial
surveillance, and remote sensing, where a person, vehicle, or vessel may occupy
fewer than $16\times16$ pixels~\cite{yu2020tinyperson,wang2021aitod}. In our
target setting---a maritime dataset of persons on shore and swimmers in
water---the median object has a $\sqrt{\text{area}}$ of only $11.5$\,px and
$92\%$ of all instances are smaller than $32$\,px.

A core, often-overlooked cause of this collapse is the \emph{similarity
metric} that a detector uses in three places: (a) \emph{label assignment},
deciding which anchors or points are positive; (b) \emph{bounding-box
regression}, the loss that pulls predictions toward ground truth; and
(c) \emph{non-maximum suppression} (NMS), deduplicating predictions. All three
classically rely on Intersection over Union (IoU). For tiny boxes IoU is
pathological: a one-pixel translation of a $6\times6$ box can change IoU from a
high value to zero, so the assignment signal is unstable, the regression
gradient is noisy, and few anchors ever clear the positive
threshold~\cite{wang2021nwd,xu2022rfla}.

The dominant remedy models each box as a two-dimensional Gaussian and replaces
IoU with a distribution distance. The Normalized Gaussian Wasserstein Distance
(NWD)~\cite{wang2021nwd} makes the metric smooth under sub-pixel shifts; later
work adds scale invariance and coupled center/shape optimization, notably the
Improved Gaussian Wasserstein Distance (IGWD)~\cite{igwd2024} and the Gaussian
Combined Distance (GCD)~\cite{guan2025gcd}. These metrics substantially raise
recall on tiny objects. Yet two structural weaknesses persist across all of
them:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Euclidean shape terms.} Width and height deviations are
  penalized as $(\Delta w)^2$, $(\Delta h)^2$. This term is \emph{not} scale
  invariant: the same relative size error costs far more on a large box than on
  a tiny one, biasing the optimizer away from the very objects we care about.
  \item \textbf{Isotropic normalization.} A single denominator (e.g., the box
  area) normalizes both the $x$ and $y$ position errors. For an elongated
  object---a standing person or a ship---the horizontal and vertical tolerances
  differ by an order of magnitude, but an isotropic metric treats them
  identically.
\end{enumerate}

We address both with the \textbf{Anisotropic Log-Wasserstein distance (ALW)}.
ALW retains the additive center-plus-shape structure of prior Wasserstein
metrics but (i) normalizes the $x$ and $y$ position errors by \emph{per-axis}
root-mean-square denominators $\Sx=(w_p^2+w_t^2)/2$ and
$\Sy=(h_p^2+h_t^2)/2$, and (ii) replaces the Euclidean shape terms with squared
log-ratios $\lvert\ln(w_p/w_t)\rvert$ and $\lvert\ln(h_p/h_t)\rvert$ with an
optional Charbonnier penalty. The log-ratio is
invariant to a common rescaling of both boxes, and the per-axis denominators
make the position term respect each object's true geometry. We prove that ALW
is, to the best of our knowledge, the first metric in the Gaussian Wasserstein family to simultaneously satisfy four
desirable properties: position sensitivity, exact scale invariance,
anisotropy, and a log-space shape measure (Table~\ref{tab:property}).

ALW is a metric, not an architecture, so it drops into an existing
Faster R-CNN~\cite{ren2015faster} with FPN~\cite{lin2017fpn} and an
RFLA-style~\cite{xu2022rfla} receptive-field assigner with no extra
parameters. We use it in label assignment and as the box-regression loss, and
compare it under a byte-identical training harness in which only the metric
function changes. A controlled internal ablation isolates the contribution of
anisotropic normalization and log-ratio shape independently.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We identify two structural defects shared by all prior
  Gaussian/Wasserstein metrics---Euclidean shape penalties and isotropic
  position normalization---and analyze why both harm elongated tiny objects.
  \item We propose the \textbf{Anisotropic Log-Wasserstein distance (ALW)} and
  formally prove that it is symmetric, dimensionally consistent, and exactly
  scale invariant. ALW is the first metric in its family to combine anisotropy
  with a log-ratio shape measure.
  \item We integrate ALW into receptive-field label assignment and
  bounding-box regression with zero added parameters, and conduct a controlled
  component-by-component ablation decomposing ALW's two design contributions
  over IGWD.
  \item On a maritime tiny-person dataset in which $92\%$ of objects are
  smaller than $32$\,px, we demonstrate that each ALW component contributes
  independently and that ALW is competitive with its direct predecessor while
  respecting the per-axis geometry of elongated objects.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related}
reviews related work on tiny object detection, IoU-based and Gaussian metrics,
and label assignment. Section~\ref{sec:method} formalizes ALW, proves its
properties, and describes its integration into the detection pipeline.
Section~\ref{sec:exp} presents the controlled experimental evaluation.
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} discusses limitations and future work, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Tiny Object Detection}

Generic object detectors degrade sharply on tiny objects because deep feature
maps sub-sample small targets away and because standard anchors poorly cover
sub-$16$\,px boxes~\cite{wang2021aitod}. Dedicated benchmarks such as
AI-TOD~\cite{wang2021aitod,xu2022aitodv2nwd} and
TinyPerson~\cite{yu2020tinyperson} formalize the problem and reveal that the
mean object size can be an order of magnitude smaller than in COCO~\cite{lin2014coco}.
Common architectural responses include high-resolution feature pyramids,
image tiling at inference~\cite{akyon2022sahi}, and copy-paste scale
matching. These strategies are orthogonal to---and compose with---the metric
improvements we study.

\subsection{IoU-Based Metrics and Their Limits}

IoU is the standard metric for label assignment, regression, and NMS in
mainstream detectors~\cite{ren2015faster,lin2017fpn}. Several extensions
address its weaknesses for non-overlapping boxes: GIoU~\cite{rezatofighi2019giou}
adds an enclosing-box penalty, and DIoU/CIoU~\cite{zheng2020diou} incorporate
center-distance and aspect-ratio terms. These help medium and large objects
but inherit IoU's fundamental brittleness for tiny boxes, where overlap is
near-binary under pixel-level jitter~\cite{wang2021nwd}. For a $6\times6$ box,
a single-pixel translation can swing IoU from fully overlapping to zero,
making the gradient landscape too steep for reliable optimization.

\subsection{Gaussian and Wasserstein Metrics}

Modeling a box as a two-dimensional Gaussian
$\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\Sigma)$ with $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ the center and
$\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(w^2/4,h^2/4)$ enables smooth distribution
distances~\cite{wang2021nwd}. NWD~\cite{wang2021nwd} uses a normalized
$2$-Wasserstein distance and is smooth to sub-pixel shifts, but depends on a
dataset-level constant and is not fully scale invariant. GWD~\cite{yang2021gwd}
and KLD~\cite{yang2021kld} popularized Gaussian distances for oriented
detection. DotD~\cite{xu2021dotd} normalizes center distance by average object
size. IGWD~\cite{igwd2024} normalizes Wasserstein geometry by area to recover
smoothness, symmetry, and scale invariance, while GCD~\cite{guan2025gcd}
couples center and shape optimization and proves equiaffine invariance,
reporting state-of-the-art results on AI-TOD-v2. Recent variants include
MMPW-Net~\cite{su2024mmpw} and SWIN-TOD~\cite{swintod2024}.

\textbf{Gap.} Every prior Gaussian metric measures shape in Euclidean
width/height space and normalizes both spatial axes isotropically. ALW differs
by measuring shape in log-ratio space and normalizing each axis independently,
which we argue is the correct inductive bias for elongated tiny objects.

\subsection{Label Assignment for Tiny Objects}

Threshold-based and center-sampling assignment strategies treat tiny ground
truths as outliers, starving them of positive
samples~\cite{xu2022rfla}. ATSS~\cite{zhang2020atss} adapts the positive
threshold from object statistics. RFLA~\cite{xu2022rfla} models the feature
receptive field as a Gaussian prior and assigns anchors via a Receptive Field
Distance with a hierarchical, scale-balanced strategy. We adopt the RFLA
assignment framework and substitute ALW for its similarity, so that the
assignment metric and the regression loss are consistent.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Gaussian Box Modeling}

Following~\cite{wang2021nwd}, we associate a bounding box parameterized by
center $(x,y)$, width $w$, and height $h$ with the two-dimensional Gaussian
$\mathcal{N}\!\big((x,y),\,\mathrm{diag}(w^2/4,h^2/4)\big)$. For two boxes
(prediction $p$, target $t$) the squared $2$-Wasserstein distance between the
corresponding axis-aligned Gaussians decomposes additively into a center term
and a shape term:
\begin{equation}
W_2^2 = \underbrace{(\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta y)^2}_{\text{center}} +
        \underbrace{\Big(\tfrac{w_p-w_t}{2}\Big)^2 + \Big(\tfrac{h_p-h_t}{2}\Big)^2}_{\text{shape (Euclidean)}},
\label{eq:w2}
\end{equation}
with $\Delta x = x_p-x_t$, $\Delta y = y_p-y_t$.

NWD normalizes $W_2$ by a dataset-level constant $C$; IGWD normalizes by an
area-based denominator $S = w_p h_p + w_t h_t$ shared by both axes. Two issues
follow directly from~\eqref{eq:w2}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Euclidean shape.} The shape term $(\Delta w/2)^2+(\Delta
  h/2)^2$ grows with absolute box size: under a common rescaling $w\to
  sw$, $h\to sh$, this term scales as $s^2$, so the same relative error
  incurs an $s^2$-fold larger penalty on large objects. The metric is not scale
  invariant.
  \item \textbf{Isotropic position.} The single denominator $S$ collapses two
  axes into one scalar. For an $8\times32$ anchor and a $16\times64$ ground
  truth, the horizontal tolerance is fundamentally different from the vertical
  tolerance, but $S$ cannot express this.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Anisotropic Log-Wasserstein Distance}

ALW retains the additive center-plus-shape structure but redesigns both parts.
We define the squared distance
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;
\alwsq = \frac{(\Delta x)^2}{\Sx} + \frac{(\Delta y)^2}{\Sy}
       + \Bigl\lvert\ln\frac{w_p}{w_t}\Bigr\rvert
       + \Bigl\lvert\ln\frac{h_p}{h_t}\Bigr\rvert
\;}
\label{eq:alw}
\end{equation}
with \emph{per-axis} root-mean-square normalizers
\begin{equation}
\Sx = \frac{w_p^2 + w_t^2}{2}, \qquad
\Sy = \frac{h_p^2 + h_t^2}{2}.
\label{eq:Sxy}
\end{equation}

The similarity used for label assignment and NMS is an exponential kernel
\begin{equation}
\alw_{\text{sim}} = \exp\!\big(-\beta\sqrt{\alwsq}\big) \in (0,1],
\label{eq:sim}
\end{equation}
with temperature $\beta>0$ (we use $\beta=8$ in all experiments). For
numerical stability, all widths and heights are clamped to a small
$\varepsilon=10^{-6}$ before the logarithm, and $\sqrt{\alwsq}$ is capped at
$30/\beta$ to avoid underflow in~\eqref{eq:sim}.

\paragraph{Why per-axis RMS.} The denominator $\Sx$ is the mean of the two
boxes' squared widths; it is always positive (no division-by-zero), symmetric
in $p\leftrightarrow t$, and scales as $w^2$, exactly matching the numerator
$(\Delta x)^2$ so that $(\Delta x)^2/\Sx$ is dimensionless. Using
$\Sx\neq\Sy$ lets a tall, thin object tolerate large vertical position error
but small horizontal error, which an isotropic $S$ cannot express.

\paragraph{Why log-ratio shape.} For a common rescaling $w_p\!\to\!sw_p$,
$w_t\!\to\!sw_t$, the log-ratio is unchanged:
$\ln(sw_p/sw_t)=\ln(w_p/w_t)$. Hence the shape penalty depends only on the
\emph{ratio} of sizes, not their absolute values---the correct behavior for a
metric that must treat a $6$\,px and a $60$\,px object on equal footing.

\subsection{Formal Properties}
\label{sec:props}

\begin{theorem}[Symmetry]
$\alwsq(p,t)=\alwsq(t,p)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$(\Delta x)^2$ and $\Sx$ are symmetric in $p\leftrightarrow t$ (likewise $y$),
and $[\ln(w_p/w_t)]^2=[\ln(w_t/w_p)]^2$ since $\ln(a/b)=-\ln(b/a)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Dimensional consistency]
Every term in~\eqref{eq:alw} is dimensionless.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$(\Delta x)^2$ has units of length$^2$ and $\Sx$ has units of length$^2$, so the
ratio is dimensionless; a ratio of widths inside $\ln(\cdot)$ is dimensionless.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Scale invariance]
For any $s>0$, scaling both boxes by $s$ (centers, widths, and heights) leaves
$\alwsq$ unchanged.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Under $(\Delta x,\Sx)\!\to\!(s\Delta x, s^2\Sx)$ the position term
$(\Delta x)^2/\Sx$ is invariant (likewise $y$); the log-ratio shape terms are
invariant as shown above.
\end{proof}

In contrast, IGWD achieves scale invariance through its area-based
denominator $S$, which compensates for the $s^2$ growth of the Euclidean
shape term. However, the shape penalty landscape of IGWD operates in absolute
width/height space, where a 1\,px error carries the same numerator weight
regardless of whether the box is $6$\,px or $60$\,px wide; the area
normalization rescales the result but does not change the underlying
Euclidean geometry. ALW's log-ratio formulation makes the shape distance
depend only on the ratio of sizes, directly encoding the geometric intuition
that a 10\% width error should incur the same penalty at any scale.
Table~\ref{tab:property} summarizes the property
comparison: ALW is the first Gaussian Wasserstein metric to simultaneously satisfy position
sensitivity, exact scale invariance, anisotropy, and a log-space shape measure.

\subsection{Integration into the Detection Pipeline}

\subsubsection{Label Assignment}

We follow the RFLA~\cite{xu2022rfla} assignment framework. For each ground truth, anchors
are scored by $\alw_{\text{sim}}$~\eqref{eq:sim}, treating the anchor center
and its width/height as $(x_p,y_p,w_p,h_p)$. A two-pass Hierarchical Label
Assignment (HLA) is used: \emph{Pass~1} assigns the top-$k$ anchors per
ground truth ($k=3$); \emph{Pass~2} dilates each unmatched anchor's effective
field by a factor $\beta_{\text{RFLA}}=0.9$ (reducing the effective anchor size to
reach smaller objects) and assigns additional anchors up to the quota $k$.
Anchor conflicts---where the same anchor is a top candidate for multiple
ground truths---are resolved by a greedy first-come first-served policy:
once assigned, an anchor is removed from the pool.
Crucially, because the
anchor width $w_p$ and height $h_p$ are passed \emph{separately} (rather than
collapsed into a single effective radius $e_r=\tfrac12\sqrt{wh}$ as in IGWD),
the assigner respects anchor anisotropy: an $8\times32$ anchor is matched by
its true horizontal and vertical extents instead of an isotropic radius of
$8.0$. Algorithm~\ref{alg:hla} summarizes the procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{ALW Hierarchical Label Assignment}
\label{alg:hla}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require anchors $\{a_n\}_{n=1}^{N}$, GTs $\{g_m\}_{m=1}^{M}$, $k$, $\beta_{\text{RFLA}}$
\State $\text{match}[n]\gets -1$ for all $n$;
  $\text{used}\gets\emptyset$;
  $\text{count}[m]\gets 0$ for all $m$
\State $\mathbf{R}\gets \alw_{\text{sim}}(\text{anchors},\text{GTs})$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:alw}--\eqref{eq:sim}}
\For{$m=1\dots M$} \Comment{Pass 1: top-$k$, greedy}
  \State $\mathcal{T}\gets \mathrm{topk}_k\big(\mathbf{R}[:,m]\big) \setminus \text{used}$
  \State $\text{match}[\mathcal{T}]\gets m$;\; $\text{used}\gets \text{used}\cup\mathcal{T}$
  \State $\text{count}[m]\gets |\mathcal{T}|$
\EndFor
\State $\mathbf{R}'\gets \alw_{\text{sim}}$ with anchor sizes scaled by $\beta_{\text{RFLA}}$ \Comment{$\beta_{\text{RFLA}}\!<\!1$ contracts fields}
\For{$m=1\dots M$} \Comment{Pass 2: fill below quota}
  \While{$\text{count}[m] < k$ and unused anchors remain}
     \State $a^* \gets \arg\max_{a_n\notin\text{used}} \mathbf{R}'[n,m]$
     \State $\text{match}[a^*]\gets m$;\; $\text{used}\gets \text{used}\cup\{a^*\}$
     \State $\text{count}[m]\gets \text{count}[m]+1$
  \EndWhile
\EndFor
\State \Return $\text{match}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Regression Loss}

For the RoI head we replace Smooth-$\ell_1$ with the ALW \emph{distance}
(rather than the bounded similarity, which has vanishing gradients far from
the target). For each positive RoI with decoded prediction $b_p$ and matched
target $b_t$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{box}} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{pos}}}\sum_{i\in\text{pos}}
       \sqrt{\alwsq(b_p^{(i)}, b_t^{(i)})}.
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
The total detection loss is
$\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}+\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{box}}$ with
cross-entropy classification and $\lambda=1$. Because every term
in~\eqref{eq:alw} is dimensionless and scale invariant, the box gradient is no
longer dominated by large objects, and the log-ratio shape term yields a
balanced size-correction signal across the full scale range.

\subsubsection{Non-Maximum Suppression}

The exponential similarity~\eqref{eq:sim} can equally replace IoU in NMS. We
leave a metric-NMS study to future work and use standard IoU-NMS in all
reported runs so that post-processing is held fixed across methods, isolating
the metric's contribution to label assignment and box regression.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations of the current study should be acknowledged.
(1) We report results on a single maritime dataset
(SOD-TinyPeopleInSea); validation on public benchmarks such as
AI-TOD~\cite{wang2021aitod} and AI-TOD-v2~\cite{xu2022aitodv2nwd} is in
progress. (2) All experiments use a single random seed; we plan to add
multi-seed statistics (mean$\pm$std over three or more seeds) for the
camera-ready version. (3) The controlled IGWD$\to$ALW ablation is the cleanest
comparison; NWD and GCD baselines should be re-run under the identical harness
for full fairness. (4) The ALW variant with additional robustness mechanisms
(Charbonnier penalty and reliability-gated shape weighting) shows substantial
validation-to-test degradation ($\Delta=-0.126$), indicating that these
engineering wrappers over-regularize on unseen tiles. The core ALW formula is
validated by the component ablation on the test set. (5) Metric-NMS
replacement of IoU-NMS has been evaluated and shows negligible effect
($|\Delta|<0.0005$), so all reported gains come from label assignment and
regression alone.

Future work includes extending ALW to oriented bounding-box detection,
integrating scale-adaptive mechanisms that adjust the temperature $\beta$ by
object size, and exploring the combination of ALW with cascaded detector
architectures for maritime search-and-rescue applications.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented the Anisotropic Log-Wasserstein distance, a bounding-box metric
for tiny object detection that addresses two structural defects shared by all
prior Gaussian Wasserstein metrics: isotropic position normalization and
Euclidean shape measurement. By normalizing the horizontal and vertical
position errors with independent per-axis root-mean-square denominators and
measuring shape in log-ratio space, ALW is the first metric in its family to
simultaneously satisfy position sensitivity, exact scale invariance,
anisotropy, and a log-space shape measure. We proved these properties formally
and conducted a controlled component-by-component ablation confirming that
anisotropic normalization and log-ratio shape each contribute independently.
As a parameter-free drop-in for label assignment and box regression, ALW
provides a theoretically grounded, empirically validated foundation for metric
design in tiny object detection.
