\begin{abstract}
Detecting tiny objects is challenging because small localization errors can sharply reduce bounding-box overlap. Once predicted and target boxes become strictly disjoint, the plain IoU loss provides no centroid-gradient signal. We study Scale-Adaptive Wasserstein--IoU (H-WIoU), which blends an arm-specific geometric affinity with IoU through a rational gate without learned parameters, $\gamma(s)=s^2/(s^2+\sigma_0^2)$. The frozen arms use either symmetric scale-normalized divergence or target-normalized Gaussian W$_2$; the YOLO path caps the latter's exponent. For smaller targets, the objective places more weight on geometric affinity, while larger targets place more weight on IoU. For the two uncapped variants on compact, strictly disjoint box domains, we establish a positive centroid-gradient lower bound in exact real arithmetic and show that the negative centroid gradient is a descent direction for squared centroid error. We also study detached feature entropy (EH-WIoU) and apply the similarity to label assignment through HLA, H-TAL, and H-STAL.

Experiments on Scale Match TinyPerson and AI-TOD-v2 evaluate the formulation across Faster R-CNN, FCOS, Cascade R-CNN, RetinaNet, YOLOv8s, and NMS-free YOLO26s. On TinyPerson validation, Faster R-CNN dual placement increases $\mathrm{AP}_{50}^{\mathrm{tiny1}}$ from $20.35\%$ to $25.65\%$, while EH-WIoU reaches $26.46\%$; FCOS with H-WIoU and Gaussian centerness increases overall $\mathrm{AP}_{50}$ from $27.52\%$ to $31.13\%$ within its factorial protocol. Held-out TinyPerson test results include gains on the smallest-object subsets, while a further 51 extended models show results that vary by detector and metric. Across 71 canonical and extended test evaluations, these single-seed measurements do not establish a general ranking of losses. Validation and official-test scores are presented separately. The proposed terms are used only during training and introduce no additional inference-time operators. Code is available at the project repository.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Detecting tiny objects is critical for real-world applications such as maritime search and rescue, drone surveillance, and aerial satellite monitoring~\cite{yu2020tinyperson,wang2021aitod,xu2022aitodv2nwd}. In these settings, objects often occupy only a handful of pixels on the camera sensor. For example, a swimmer stranded in open water or a distant vehicle in satellite imagery may have a characteristic scale of $s = \sqrt{wh} < 8$ pixels.

At this microscopic scale, standard object detection methods face a fundamental geometric failure:
\begin{quote}
  \emph{A shift of just 1 or 2 pixels can completely eliminate bounding-box overlap.}
\end{quote}
Consider two identical $4\times4$-pixel bounding boxes. If one box is shifted by 2 pixels in both horizontal and vertical directions, the overlapping area drops from $16$ pixels down to just $4$ pixels—a $75\%$ loss of overlap, resulting in $\mathrm{IoU} = 4/(16+16-4) = 1/7 \approx 0.14$. If the shift exceeds 4 pixels, the boxes no longer overlap at all ($\mathrm{IoU} = 0$).

\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:homotopy_geometry}(a) shows how the rational scale gate changes the mixture between geometric affinity and IoU. Panel (b) translates equal $4\times4$ boxes horizontally: first contact occurs at $d=4$ pixels, and strict separation begins for $d>4$; the GIoU curve is not clipped. Panel (c) shows one-sided translation derivatives on smooth intervals, with unequal slopes for IoU and its mixture at contact. The plot includes overlap and contact, whereas the theorem's uniform bound applies only on its compact, strictly separated domain.

Inside a strictly disjoint region, the IoU term is locally constant with respect to box coordinates, so its box-coordinate gradient is zero. Other training losses can still update the network; the missing signal here concerns the IoU regression term.

To address this problem, several geometry-extended losses have been proposed. Generalized IoU (GIoU)~\cite{rezatofighi2019generalized} adds a penalty based on the smallest enclosing box, and Distance-IoU (DIoU)~\cite{zheng2020distance} penalizes the distance between box centers. Their geometric penalties differ from the scale-conditioned affinity studied here; this paper compares the resulting detectors empirically rather than asserting a universal gradient direction for those losses.

Another approach is to model bounding boxes as 2D Gaussian distributions and measure their distance using the Normalized Wasserstein Distance (NWD)~\cite{wang2021nwd}. Its distance remains defined for separated boxes and provides a smooth center-distance term. A zero center displacement, however, has zero center gradient, and finite-precision exponentials can saturate at large distances. We blend geometric affinity with IoU so the weight assigned to each depends on target scale.

\paragraph{Our Insight: Scale-Adaptive Blending.}
We do not need to choose between IoU and Wasserstein distance. Instead, we can combine their strengths:
\begin{itemize}
  \item For \textbf{tiny objects}, place more weight on a smooth, scale-normalized geometric affinity that remains positive at finite separation in exact arithmetic.
  \item For \textbf{larger objects}, rely primarily on standard IoU to ensure sharp, tight bounding boxes.
\end{itemize}

We realize this concept in \textbf{Scale-Adaptive Wasserstein--IoU (H-WIoU)} (Figure~\ref{fig:homotopy_geometry}). Using a quadratic rational gate $\gamma(s) = s^2 / (s^2 + \sigma_0^2)$, H-WIoU shifts mixture weight toward IoU as target scale grows without adding learned gate parameters; the pivot $\sigma_0$ is selected by protocol. The frozen implementations use either a symmetric scale-normalized divergence or target-normalized Gaussian Wasserstein affinity, as specified below. We also study an entropy-guided extension (\textbf{EH-WIoU}) and apply scale-adaptive similarity to positive label assignment (\textbf{HLA}, \textbf{H-TAL}, and \textbf{H-STAL}).

\paragraph{Key Contributions:}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Scale-Adaptive Formulation.} We study H-WIoU as a family that combines one of two specified geometric affinities with IoU through a rational gate controlled by target scale, with no learned gate parameters.
  \item \textbf{Analytical Properties.} On compact, strictly disjoint box domains, we establish a uniform positive centroid-gradient lower bound in exact real arithmetic and show that the negative centroid gradient is a descent direction for squared centroid error.
  \item \textbf{Detector Integration and Factorial Analysis.} We integrate the similarity into regression and assignment across Faster R-CNN, FCOS, Cascade R-CNN, RetinaNet, YOLOv8s, and YOLO26s, and use factorial experiments to separate loss, centerness, sampling, and placement effects.
  \item \textbf{Held-Out Evaluation.} We evaluate selected configurations on the official TinyPerson test split and report detector-dependent gains and trade-offs across fine-grained object-scale subsets.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Bounding-Box Regression Losses}
Early object detectors used Smooth-L1 loss to penalize coordinate offsets independently~\cite{girshick2015fast}. Because independent coordinate regression does not optimize overall box overlap, IoU loss was introduced to directly align training with evaluation metrics. To handle disjoint boxes where IoU is zero, GIoU~\cite{rezatofighi2019generalized} incorporates the area of the smallest enclosing box, DIoU~\cite{zheng2020distance} adds a normalized center-distance penalty, and CIoU~\cite{zheng2020distance} / EIoU~\cite{zhang2022eiou} add aspect-ratio and dimension penalties. Other variants, such as $\alpha$-IoU~\cite{he2021alphaiou}, Wise-IoU~\cite{tong2023wiou}, SIoU~\cite{ghevondean2022siou}, and Inner-IoU~\cite{zhang2023inneriou}, reshape gradient weights or use auxiliary scaling factors.

In parallel, Wasserstein-based losses model bounding boxes as 2D Gaussians. Normalized Wasserstein Distance (NWD)~\cite{wang2021nwd,xu2022aitodv2nwd} computes a transport-based similarity that remains defined when boxes do not overlap. Subsequent works, including GWD~\cite{yang2021gwd}, KLD~\cite{yang2021kld}, IGWD~\cite{hu2025cfenet}, and GCD~\cite{guan2025gcd}, extended Gaussian modeling to oriented boxes and aerial imagery. H-WIoU blends an identified geometric affinity with IoU as a continuous function of object scale.

\subsection{Label Assignment for Tiny Objects}
Assigning positive training anchors is notoriously difficult for tiny objects. Standard Max-IoU matching requires candidates to exceed fixed overlap thresholds (e.g., $0.5$ or $0.7$), which microscopic objects rarely achieve. Adaptive strategies such as ATSS~\cite{zhang2020atss}, PAA~\cite{kim2020paa}, and OTA~\cite{ge2021ota} compute statistical or optimal-transport assignments. For tiny objects specifically, RFLA~\cite{xu2022rfla} uses Gaussian receptive-field points, and SimD~\cite{shi2024simd} combines centroid distance with shape similarity. Our Homotopy Label Assignment (HLA) incorporates scale-adaptive similarity directly into the anchor-matching stage.

\subsection{Real-Time and NMS-Free Detectors}
Modern one-stage detectors like YOLOv8~\cite{jocher2023yolo} and YOLOv9~\cite{wang2024yolov9} use the Task-Aligned Assigner (TAL)~\cite{feng2021tood}, which ranks candidates by $t = s^\alpha \cdot \mathrm{IoU}^\beta$. End-to-end NMS-free models, such as YOLO26s~\cite{ultralytics2026yolo26} (built upon dual-label assignment~\cite{wang2024yolov10}), eliminate non-maximum suppression by combining one-to-one and one-to-many matching branches. In this paper, we extend our scale-adaptive similarity into both YOLOv8s (H-TAL) and YOLO26s (H-STAL).


\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Representing Boxes as 2D Gaussians}
Let a predicted box be $A = (\mu_a, w_a, h_a)$ and its target be $B = (\mu_b, w_b, h_b)$, where $\mu = (x, y)^T$ is the box center, and $w, h > 0$ are the width and height. Following NWD~\cite{wang2021nwd}, we can associate each box with a 2D Gaussian distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$, where the covariance matrix is defined by:
\begin{equation}
  \Sigma = \operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{w^2}{4}, \frac{h^2}{4}\right).
\end{equation}
This converts a crisp, hard-edged rectangle into a smooth, continuous bell curve. The 2-Wasserstein distance between two such Gaussian distributions is:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:wasserstein}
  \mathcal{W}_2^2(\mathcal{N}_a, \mathcal{N}_b)
  = \|\mu_a - \mu_b\|_2^2 + \frac{(w_a - w_b)^2 + (h_a - h_b)^2}{4}.
\end{equation}
Notice that this distance has physical units of pixels squared. A 2-pixel error on a 4-pixel object has the same numerical penalty as a 2-pixel error on a 100-pixel object, even though a 2-pixel shift is massive for the tiny object ($50\%$ of its size) and negligible for the large object ($2\%$). To fix this, we must normalize the distance by scale.

\subsection{Geometric Affinities Used by the Frozen Arms}
One implementation defines the \textbf{Scale-Normalized Geometric Divergence (SNGD)} by normalizing center displacements with both boxes' dimensions and measuring shape by log ratios:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:sngd}
  \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SN}}^2(A, B)
  = \frac{2(x_a - x_b)^2}{w_a^2 + w_b^2}
  + \frac{2(y_a - y_b)^2}{h_a^2 + h_b^2}
  + \ln^2\left(\frac{w_a}{w_b}\right)
  + \ln^2\left(\frac{h_a}{h_b}\right).
\end{equation}
This divergence has several appealing properties:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Scale Invariance:} If we scale both boxes by a constant factor $\lambda > 0$ (e.g., zooming in on an image), $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SN}}^2$ remains completely unchanged.
  \item \textbf{Non-negativity:} $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SN}}^2(A, B) \ge 0$, and equals $0$ if and only if $A = B$.
  \item \textbf{Symmetry:} $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SN}}^2(A, B) = \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SN}}^2(B, A)$.
\end{itemize}

Its affinity is bounded in $(0, 1]$ via the exponential map:
\begin{equation}
  T_{\mathrm{SN}}(A, B) = \exp\left(-\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SN}}^2(A, B)\right).
\end{equation}
The other implementation normalizes the squared Gaussian Wasserstein distance in Eq.~\eqref{eq:wasserstein} by the \emph{target} area. With $s_B^2=w_bh_b$ and numerical stabilizer $\varepsilon>0$, it computes
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:target_w2}
  \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{TW}}^2(A,B)
  = \frac{\mathcal{W}_2^2(\mathcal{N}_a,\mathcal{N}_b)}{2s_B^2+\varepsilon},
  \qquad T_{\mathrm{TW}}(A,B)=\exp\!\left(-\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{TW}}^2(A,B)\right).
\end{equation}
Unlike SNGD, this target-normalized affinity uses squared width and height differences and is asymmetric in candidate and target. Both affinities equal one for identical boxes and remain positive at finite displacement in exact arithmetic. YOLO's implementation caps the exponent at 30 for numerical stability; that cap changes the gradient at extreme separations. We write $T_q$ for the exact affinity $q\in\{\mathrm{SN},\mathrm{TW}\}$ selected by the frozen arm and identify the YOLO capped variant separately.

\subsection{The Rational Scale Gate}
To blend the selected affinity $T_q(A, B)$ with standard $\mathrm{IoU}(A, B)$, we define a scale-dependent gating function. Let $s_B = \sqrt{w_b h_b}$ denote the scale of the target box, and let $\sigma_0 > 0$ be a reference scale pivot (in pixels). We define the rational gate $\gamma(s_B)$ as:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:gamma}
  \gamma(s_B) = \frac{s_B^2}{s_B^2 + \sigma_0^2}, \qquad
  1 - \gamma(s_B) = \frac{\sigma_0^2}{s_B^2 + \sigma_0^2}.
\end{equation}
This gate behaves in an intuitive and desirable way:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item For \textbf{microscopic objects} ($s_B \to 0$), $\gamma(s_B) \to 0$ and $1 - \gamma(s_B) \to 1$. The supervision relies entirely on the selected geometric affinity $T_q(A, B)$.
  \item For \textbf{large objects} ($s_B \gg \sigma_0$), $\gamma(s_B) \to 1$ and $1 - \gamma(s_B) \to 0$. The supervision transitions completely to standard $\mathrm{IoU}(A, B)$.
  \item At the \textbf{pivot scale} ($s_B = \sigma_0$), $\gamma(\sigma_0) = 0.5$, providing an equal 50/50 blend.
\end{enumerate}

For either identified affinity, the \textbf{H-WIoU similarity} and its corresponding regression loss are:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:similarity}
  \begin{split}
    \mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{H},q}(A, B) &= \gamma(s_B) \cdot \mathrm{IoU}(A, B) + \left(1 - \gamma(s_B)\right) \cdot T_q(A, B),\\
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{H},q}(A, B) &= 1 - \mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{H},q}(A, B).
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
Both $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{H},q}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{H},q}$ are bounded within $[0, 1]$. On strictly separated boxes with nonzero center displacement, the uncapped affinities supply a nonzero center-gradient component in exact arithmetic even though the IoU component is locally constant.

\subsection{Optional Feature Entropy Extension (EH-WIoU)}
\label{sec:entropy_math}
In complex backgrounds (such as sea clutter or aerial shadows), we can optionally modulate the gate using the channel entropy of local deep features. For a feature vector at spatial location $q$ with $C_f>1$ channels, we compute its normalized channel entropy $H_q \in [0, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
  p_{q,c} = \frac{e^{f_{q,c}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{C_f} e^{f_{q,j}}}, \qquad
  H_q = -\frac{\sum_{c=1}^{C_f} p_{q,c} \ln p_{q,c}}{\ln C_f}.
\end{equation}
We then modulate the scale gate using a coupling factor $\beta \ge 0$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:entropy_homotopy}
  \gamma_\beta(s_B, H) = \frac{s_B^2(1 + \beta H)}{s_B^2(1 + \beta H) + \sigma_0^2}.
\end{equation}
When $\beta = 0$, this recovers the scale-only gate for the \emph{same selected affinity} $q$. During backpropagation, detaching entropy stops gradients through the entropy-prior computation; the regression or assignment loss can still update shared detector parameters through other paths.

\subsection{Theoretical Guarantees}

\begin{theorem}[Disjoint-box centroid gradient]
  \label{thm:nonvanishing}
  Fix a target box $B$ with $w_b,h_b>0$ and $\sigma_0>0$. Define
  \begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{Z}_B=\{A:\mathrm{IoU}(A,B)=0\}.
  \end{equation*}
  For $0<\delta\le\kappa<\infty$ and $0<r_{\min}\le r_{\max}<\infty$, let $K$ be a nonempty compact subset of $\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{Z}_B)$ such that every $A\in K$ satisfies
  \begin{equation}
    \delta \le \frac{\|\mu_a - \mu_b\|_2}{s_B} \le \kappa, \qquad
    \frac{w_a}{w_b} \in [r_{\min}, r_{\max}], \qquad
    \frac{h_a}{h_b} \in [r_{\min}, r_{\max}].
  \end{equation}
  For either \emph{uncapped}, scale-only affinity $q\in\{\mathrm{SN},\mathrm{TW}\}$, in exact real arithmetic there exists a constant $c_q > 0$, depending on $B$, the stated bounds, $\sigma_0$, and $q$, such that
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:lower_bound}
    \|\nabla_{\mu_a} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{H},q}(A, B)\|_2 \ge c_q
  \end{equation}
  uniformly for all $A \in K$. This statement does not cover YOLO's capped exponent or finite-precision saturation.
\end{theorem}

\noindent\textbf{Intuition of Proof.} For $A \in K \subset \mathrm{int}(\mathcal{Z}_B)$, the boxes are strictly disjoint, so the IoU term is locally constant. Differentiating the two uncapped affinities with respect to the predicted center gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:centroid_gradient}
  \begin{aligned}
  g_{\mathrm{SN}} &= 4(1-\gamma)T_{\mathrm{SN}}
  \begin{bmatrix} (x_a-x_b)/(w_a^2+w_b^2) \\ (y_a-y_b)/(h_a^2+h_b^2) \end{bmatrix},\\
  g_{\mathrm{TW}} &= \frac{2(1-\gamma)T_{\mathrm{TW}}}{2s_B^2+\varepsilon}(\mu_a-\mu_b).
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Because $1-\gamma>0$, each uncapped $T_q>0$, and $\|\mu_a-\mu_b\|_2\ge\delta s_B>0$ with bounded positive dimensions, each continuous gradient norm has a positive minimum on compact $K$. Figure~\ref{fig:homotopy_geometry}(c) illustrates one translation slice of the SNGD variant; it does not plot the uniform constant $c_q$. \hfill $\square$

\begin{proposition}[Corrective Gradient Direction]
  \label{prop:corrective_direction}
  For either uncapped scale-only affinity $q\in\{\mathrm{SN},\mathrm{TW}\}$, any strictly disjoint differentiable box $A$ with $\Delta=\mu_a-\mu_b\ne 0$ has a centroid gradient $g_q=\nabla_{\mu_a}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{H},q}$ satisfying
  \begin{equation}
    \langle g_q,\Delta\rangle>0.
  \end{equation}
  Each expression in Eq.~\eqref{eq:centroid_gradient} is a positive coefficient times the displacement, with positive coordinate weights for SNGD. Thus $-g_q$ is a strict descent direction for squared centroid error. A centroid-only update
  \begin{equation}
    \mu_a^+ = \mu_a - \alpha g_q
  \end{equation}
  reduces the squared centroid error for sufficiently small $\alpha > 0$. This statement concerns the box-center variables and does not guarantee improvement under an arbitrary network-parameter update.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Detector Integration}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includesvg[width=0.95\textwidth,inkscapelatex=false]{figures/fig2_architecture_publication}
  \caption{H-WIoU formulations and detector integration.}
  \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}, the frozen arms use different affinities and training placements. FCOS, the Faster R-CNN placement and beta factorials, Cascade R-CNN, and RetinaNet use the target-normalized W$_2$ path. YOLO uses its capped target-normalized path. The generic Faster R-CNN metric registry can instead select SNGD under \texttt{h\_wiou}; each reported arm must be identified from its frozen command and config. These training changes leave the compared inference architectures unchanged:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RPN Label Assignment (HLA):} In Faster R-CNN, the identified $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{H},q}$ replaces the standard IoU score used during anchor matching. This can change candidate ranking and threshold crossings while retaining the detector's remaining matching rules.
  \item \textbf{RoI / Dense Box Regression:} The identified $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{H},q}$ replaces the configured baseline box loss in Faster R-CNN, FCOS, Cascade R-CNN, or RetinaNet, with placement specified per arm.
  \item \textbf{Task-Aligned Assignment (H-TAL \& H-STAL):} In one-stage YOLOv8s and end-to-end NMS-free YOLO26s, the alignment score $t = s^\alpha \cdot \mathrm{IoU}^\beta$ is replaced with:
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:htal}
    t_{\mathrm{H}} = s^\alpha \cdot \left[\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{H},q}(A, B)\right]^\beta.
  \end{equation}
  For finite distances below YOLO's exponent cap, this avoids forcing the spatial component to zero solely because IoU is zero.
\end{itemize}


\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Datasets and Evaluation Metrics}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Scale Match TinyPerson~\cite{yu2020tinyperson}:} A benchmark for tiny person detection in maritime and seaside environments. We use the audited Scale Match surface with $628$ training and $118$ validation images, tiled into $512\times512$ patches with $64$-pixel overlap ($1,684$ validation tiles). We evaluate overall AP at IoU thresholds of $0.25$, $0.50$, and $0.75$, along with fine-grained scale subsets: $\mathrm{AP}_{50}^{\mathrm{tiny1}}$ ($s \le 8$\,px) and reasonable-scale objects ($\mathrm{AP}_{50}^{\mathrm{reas.}}$).
  \item \textbf{AI-TOD-v2~\cite{xu2022aitodv2nwd}:} The dataset authors report $28,036$ aerial images across the released splits. Our frozen training contract contains $11,214$ training annotation records ($11,204$ nonempty workload images) and $2,804$ allowed validation images; the held-out final split is evaluated only in the separately audited official-test results. We report $\mathrm{AP}$ (averaged across $\text{IoU} \in [0.50:0.05:0.95]$), $\mathrm{AP}_{50}$, $\mathrm{AP}_{75}$, very-tiny and tiny-object AP ($\mathrm{AP}_{vt}$ and $\mathrm{AP}_{t}$), small-object $\mathrm{AP}_s$, and recall $\mathrm{AR}_{1500}$.
\end{itemize}
All metrics in this paper are reported in \textbf{percentages (\%)} with two decimal places, and IoU thresholds are ordered in ascending sequence ($\mathrm{AP}_{25} \to \mathrm{AP}_{50} \to \mathrm{AP}_{75}$).

\subsection{Implementation and Training Protocols}
All experiments use PyTorch and Seed 42. Training runs were executed on Kaggle GPUs; an NVIDIA RTX 5070 Ti was used for local checkpoint replay and latency measurements. Two-stage and dense anchor-free models use ResNet-50-FPN backbones~\cite{he2016resnet,lin2017fpn}. Model parameters are: Faster R-CNN ($41.3$M), FCOS ($32.1$M), Cascade R-CNN ($41.9$M), RetinaNet ($32.2$M), YOLOv8s ($11.1$M), and YOLO26s ($9.9$M). The TinyPerson Faster R-CNN, FCOS, Cascade R-CNN, and RetinaNet comparisons use 20-epoch training budgets; family-specific optimizer settings are recorded in their frozen protocols. AI-TOD-v2 FCOS loss comparisons use 10 epochs, whereas the Faster R-CNN label-assignment comparison uses 12 epochs. YOLOv8s and YOLO26s request a maximum of 100 epochs with early-stopping patience 25; some runs end earlier.

\subsection{Entropy Coupling Factor ($\beta$)}
\label{sec:beta_sweep}
Sweeping the entropy coupling parameter $\beta \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}$ in Faster R-CNN yields overall $\mathrm{AP}_{50}$ values of $42.23\%$, $42.39\%$, $42.48\%$, $42.47\%$, and $42.97\%$, with corresponding tiny1 values of $26.32\%$, $24.92\%$, $22.59\%$, $24.52\%$, and $26.46\%$. Setting $\beta = 1.0$ improves overall $\mathrm{AP}_{50}$ by $+0.74$ pp over the uncoupled $\beta = 0$ reference, while reasonable-scale AP increases from $56.62\%$ to $59.78\%$ ($+3.16$ pp).


\section{Discussion}
Across the reported validation and official-test comparisons, some H-WIoU configurations improve tiny-object scores, while changes in aggregate scores depend on the detector and training setup. On the official test splits, TinyPerson FCOS with the full bundle has higher tiny1 AP50 than its baseline, but the Cascade R-CNN comparison has slightly lower overall AP50. On AI-TOD-v2, the FCOS H-WIoU model improves overall AP over its baseline but has a lower very-tiny AP. These single-seed results describe the tested checkpoints; they do not establish a universal advantage or explain why a difference occurs.


\section{Limitations and Boundaries}
H-WIoU has several important boundaries. First, the centroid-gradient lower bound applies only to the uncapped SNGD and target-normalized W$_2$ affinities on compact, strictly disjoint box domains in exact real arithmetic; it is not a global, scale-uniform guarantee and does not imply convergence of the full detector. It does not cover YOLO's capped exponent or numerical saturation. Second, all reported experiments use a single random seed (42), so run-to-run variance and statistical significance are not estimated. Third, SNGD uses logarithms while the target-normalized W$_2$ route does not; both use an exponential affinity and add training-time computation, which is not separately benchmarked here. Finally, the empirical effect is detector- and configuration-dependent: several experiments show improvements on tiny-object subsets together with neutral or negative changes in aggregate metrics.


\section{Conclusion}
We studied Scale-Adaptive Wasserstein--IoU (H-WIoU) as a family that blends an arm-specific scale-normalized geometric affinity with IoU using a target-scale-dependent gate. The gradient result is limited to the uncapped SNGD and target-normalized W$_2$ variants on the compact, strictly disjoint box domain stated in the analysis. We applied the identified similarities to regression and label assignment in several detector families.

Across 71 official-test evaluations from TinyPerson and AI-TOD-v2, the observed effects vary by detector and metric. Several tested configurations score higher on selected tiny-object metrics, while other aggregate scores are unchanged or lower. In the single-seed TinyPerson homotopy-form comparison, the sigmoid form had higher validation AP, AP$_{50}$, and tiny1 AP$_{50}$ than the rational form. The results support further study of scale-conditioned box similarity, but do not establish that the rational gate is the best choice or that H-WIoU improves every detector. Since all experiments use Seed 42, run-to-run variation is not measured. The proposed terms are used during training and add no inference-time operators.
